# Advanced Endodontic Microsurgery: Eradicating Internal Bacterial Infection to Preserve Natural Dentition

Deep within every natural tooth lies the dental pulp, a specialized neurovascular tissue containing nerves, blood vessels, and connective tissue. When extensive dental decay, traumatic fractures, or deep cracks breach the protective enamel and dentine shields, oral bacteria invade the pulp chamber. This bacterial invasion triggers severe pulp inflammation, throbbing toothaches, and eventually progressive pulp necrosis.

Pulp necrosis also deprives the tooth of internal immune circulation, leaving the root canals vulnerable to anaerobic bacterial colonization. If left untreated, bacterial toxins seep through microscopic apical foramina into the surrounding alveolar bone. This chronic infection causes localized bone destruction, acute facial swelling, and debilitating systemic inflammation.

Historically, severe pulp infection left clinicians with few options other than extracting the compromised tooth. However, modern endodontic therapy allows dentists to eliminate internal bacterial infection, disinfect intricate root canal systems, and seal root spaces hermetically. Saving natural teeth preserves jawbone architecture and maintains optimal masticatory chewing efficiency.

## Clinical Indications and Management of Impacted Third Molars

Wisdom teeth, or third molars, represent the final set of teeth to erupt into the dental arch, typically emerging between seventeen and twenty-five years of age. Due to evolutionary reductions in modern human jaw size, many individuals lack sufficient space to accommodate these late-emerging teeth. As a result, wisdom teeth frequently become partially or fully impacted within the jawbone.

Impacted wisdom teeth can erupt at abnormal angles, pushing against the roots of adjacent second molars and causing structural resorption. Partially erupted wisdom teeth create loose gum flaps known as opercula, which trap food particles and oral bacteria. This trapped debris leads to recurring, painful gum infections known as pericoronitis, characterized by jaw stiffness, facial swelling, and bad breath.

## Postoperative Healing Protocols and Dry Socket Prevention

This blood clot protects the exposed bone and nerve endings while serving as a biological scaffold for new bone cell migration. If the blood clot dislodges prematurely, the patient experiences an uncomfortable condition known as alveolar osteitis, or dry socket.

To prevent dry socket, clinicians advise patients to avoid smoking, drinking through straws, and vigorous mouth rinsing for the first forty-eight hours. Applying gentle external ice packs during the initial twenty-four hours minimizes facial swelling, while prescribed analgesics manage localized discomfort effectively. Within several days, soft granulation tissue lines the socket, followed by gradual bone remodeling over subsequent months.
